Tokyo Trains

Tokyo by train,
one tap at a time

One prepaid card, tapped at the gate, covers nearly every train, subway and bus in the city. This is the version I give my guests: how to get the card, how to pass the gates, and how to find the right train. Everything is checked against the operators' official pages, with sources linked.

Short answer: get one Suica, and tap.

One IC card is all you need. With a Suica or a PASMO you tap through the gates on JR trains, on both subways, and on most private railways and buses in Tokyo. No fare charts, no paper tickets, nothing to insert. The same card also pays at convenience stores, vending machines and station lockers.

1 tapTouch the card to the blue reader for about a second, going in and coming out
¥500 or noneDeposit on a plain Suica or PASMO. The visitor cards below carry no deposit
¥20,000The most any of these cards can hold at one time

One honest warning: a single ride has to start and finish inside the same IC card area. JR East prints that rule on its own Welcome Suica page. Inside Tokyo, and on day trips out of it, you will never meet the rule — but one card is not a way to ride across regions.

Through the gate in three moves

The gates look strict and are actually simple. Look for these signs.

改札Ticket gate
精算機Fare adjustment machine
出口Exit

If the gate beeps and stays shut, do not push through. Low balance is the usual reason: top up at the fare adjustment machine by the gates before you try again. A card buried in a holder with other cards, or a gate that misread you last time, are the other causes JR East lists — the staffed gate at the end of the row fixes all of them.

Three ways to get your card

Same tap at the gate, three different cards. Pick by how long you are staying.

On your phone, no queue

Welcome Suica Mobile

JR East's official app for visitors, in English. It puts a Suica on your iPhone, so there is nothing to queue for and no deposit to pay.

  • Download the app and issue the card inside it. iPhone on iOS 17.2 or later, or a recent Apple Watch.
  • Top up inside the app with Apple Pay. The balance is capped at ¥20,000, like every Suica.
  • It stays valid for 180 days from the day you issue it.
  • Tap the phone at the gate exactly like a card. No need to open the app.

Two honest catches: there is no Android version, and the balance cannot be refunded. JR East also warns that in some countries the app can only be set up after you arrive in Japan. A plain Suica can live in Apple Wallet instead — the app is simply the one built for a short trip.

At the airport or a big station

Welcome Suica, the red card

The visitor-only plastic card, sold by JR East. In principle one per person, and you can take it home as a souvenir afterwards.

  • Sold at JR EAST Travel Service Centers — Narita Airport, Haneda Airport Terminal 3, Tokyo, Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro, Ueno and more — and at the red Welcome Suica vending machines.
  • Choose ¥1,000, ¥2,000, ¥3,000, ¥4,000, ¥5,000 or ¥10,000. Every yen is spendable: there is no deposit.
  • Valid for 28 days counting the day you buy it. It dies on day 29.
  • The balance is never refunded, so load little and top up as you go.
  • Top-ups are cash only, at ticket machines and fare adjustment machines. A credit card cannot charge this card.
  • Keep the reference paper that comes with it. Your real expiry date is printed there — the date stamped on the card itself is not it.
Staying longer, or coming back

A plain Suica or PASMO

The standard cards residents use. No 28-day clock, and worth it if Tokyo is not a one-time trip.

  • A ¥500 deposit is included in the first price you pay at the machine.
  • Suica comes from JR East station ticket machines. PASMO comes from subway and private-railway station machines and counters — each card is sold by its own operators, not at JR stations.
  • The card only expires after 10 years without use.
  • Leaving for good? Hand the card back — Suica at a JR East station, PASMO at a PASMO operator — and your balance comes back minus a ¥220 handling fee, with the ¥500 deposit returned in full.
Riding with children?
  • JR East's rule: children aged 6 and over need a ticket or a card of their own. One card taps one person through.
  • Ages 6 to 11 ride at child fares, roughly half price. From 12, the adult fare applies.
  • Child versions of the cards exist — Welcome Suica is sold in adult and child versions — so ask at the same counter or machine.

Finding your train

You do not need to read Japanese to do this. You need a color, a letter and a number.

のりかえTransfer
出口Exit
女性専用車Women-only car

Every subway line carries a color and a letter, and every station on it carries a number. The circle on the platform sign tells you all three at a glance.

LineMarkColor
Ginza LineGOrange
Marunouchi LineMRed
Hanzomon LineZPurple
Toei Asakusa LineARose red
JR Yamanote LineJY (a square, not a circle)Green
  • Match the letter and the color, not the name. The station name in Japanese is the last thing to check, not the first.
  • Numbers climb in one direction. Standing at Z04 and heading for Z12, you want the platform where the numbers go up. The sign at the end of the platform lists the next stations that way.
  • Direction means destination. Trains are named by where they end up. The departure board and the front of the train show that last station — and so does Google Maps, which is why I tell guests to plan the route there and then simply match what the station shows.
  • JR wears squares. The Yamanote loop is JY in a green square, and it touches nearly every central station. If you can find the loop, you can find your way home.
  • At the far end, exits beat station names. Big stations have dozens of exits. Google Maps names the best one; the yellow exit signs inside the station will walk you to it.

Changing trains

Most changes are a walk, not a new ticket. Your card handles the money.

  • Same station, same company: follow the color of your next line. You stay inside the gates the whole way.
  • JR to subway, or subway to JR: look for the transfer gate, often orange, and tap again as you pass it. The two fares are settled together on your card.
  • Big stations are small hikes. Changing lines inside Shinjuku, Ikebukuro or Tokyo can take several minutes of walking. My advice: give yourself five spare minutes there, and the change stops feeling like a race.

Why I say "spare minutes": that is my advice as your guide, not an official rule. The signs will get you there either way — the cushion is for your nerves, and for the coffee you can now stop for.

Small things that make the ride easy

None of these are laws. All of them are how the city quietly agrees to share the trains.

Priority seats and your phone
  • Priority seats sit by the doors, marked, and are meant for older riders, people with disabilities, pregnant riders and anyone with a baby. Sit in one if it is free — and give it up the moment someone who needs it boards.
  • Set your phone to silent and do not take calls inside the car. Texting is fine.
  • Near the priority seats during rush hour, both subway operators ask you to switch the phone off completely.
On the platform, queue like you mean it
  • The marks on the platform floor show where the doors will stop. Queue in the lines either side of them.
  • When the train arrives, let everyone off first. Then board and move inside, away from the doors.
  • Backpacks come off your back in a crowded car — hold yours in front of you or between your feet.
Rush hour is real
  • Weekday mornings, roughly 7:30 to 9:30, are the squeeze. Evenings are busy too, but kinder.
  • If your day is flexible, ride after 10 in the morning. The same trip feels like a different city.
  • With a big suitcase or a stroller, that one choice — avoiding the peak — does more for your trip than any other tip on this page.
Last trains come early
  • Tokyo goes to bed early for a big city: most lines finish around midnight, and there is no night service behind them.
  • The exact last train differs by station and line. Check it in your route app before a late dinner, not after.
  • Miss it and the options are a taxi or a long walk — both are more expensive than the glance at the timetable.
Big suitcases on city trains
  • City trains have no baggage reservation and no luggage car — that system belongs to the Shinkansen. A suitcase rides free, and it stays with you.
  • Stand it by the doors, keep the aisle clear, and travel outside rush hour when you can.
  • Station coin lockers are the better answer for a free afternoon: many of them take the same IC card as payment and as the key.
The women-only car
  • On weekday mornings, some lines set one car aside for women. Tokyo Metro runs them from the first train until about 9:30; Toei runs them on the Shinjuku and Oedo lines.
  • Pink signs on the platform floor and on the car show where it stops and when it applies. Children and passengers who need a caregiver can ride too.
  • Outside the signed hours, the car is open to everyone.

What I could not confirm

I left these out instead of guessing.

  • Whether an Android phone bought outside Japan can carry a Suica. The visitor app is iPhone only, and JR East's English pages do not promise foreign Android phones a card. If your phone is Android, plan on plastic.
  • Whether your own bank card will work for top-ups in the app or in Apple Wallet. That sits between you and your card issuer. Carry some cash: the station machines that charge a physical card take cash anyway.
  • Today's stock of Welcome Suica at each sales point. JR East lists where it is sold and limits it to one per person, but I cannot see the shelves from here.
  • The exact last train from your station. It changes by station and by line, so the departure board and your route app are the source of truth — not this page.

If anything on this page fails you at a gate, the staffed gate at the end of the row is the answer. Show the attendant your card and this page, and it will get sorted.
